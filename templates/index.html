An Introduction to Mangroves

Mangrove forests are some of the most underrated biomes ever. Despite covering a very low portion of earth’s land area (roughly 0.01%), mangrove forests play crucial roles in the world. This website will explore the ways mangrove forests impact human cultures, food habits and transportation. It will also cover settlement patterns, environmental impact and the challenges people face in this environment. In this site, we will specifically focus on two mangrove regions. Our focus will be on the Sundarbans, and the Madagascar mangroves.

Cultures


The communities in both the Sunderbans and Madagascar are heavily reliant on the resources provided by the mangroves. In the Sunderbans the people believe in the balance of nature and they try to take only what they need. Shukumar Mondol said, “Tigers kill people, but I don't believe they’re angry at humans. It’s probably our own fault." An unnamed person from the Bay of Assassins in Madagascar, said "If there wasn't a mangrove forest when a cyclone hits, the water would've risen higher than other times. The mangroves protect us during times of high water."



Food


The food in both communities is mainly composed of seafood such as shrimp, prawns, crab and fish. In the Sundarbans the food is often served on banana leaves and each meal is made from fresh, local resources. Deepa Girish said, “Every meal was simple yet bursting with flavor”. Some popular dishes in the Sunderbans include, Bhapa Ilish (steamed Hilsa), Chingri Malai Curry (Prawn curry with coconut milk), Crab Masala, and Patishapta (sweet pancakes). In Madagascar, the cuisine is more or less the same, just with different names, and variations of spices



Challenges

The challenges that people in the mangroves face are mainly from the wildlife and weather. In the Sundarbans, Bengal Tigers and other various animals act as a threat to humans. Other threats and challenges include mangroves being destroyed. While mangrove deforestation provides benefits for one’s livelihood, it can be detrimental to the environment, which will affect us in the long run. In coastal areas, mangrove forests act as shields from cyclones, keeping the water levels lower. In Madagascar, and many other village communities, poverty is also a major societal challenge. The daily income for the average person in Madagascar is a mere 2 USD per day, which means people need to take advantage of nature, to continue with their life.

Transportation


In both communities, transportation is mostly by foot, as there aren't as many roads suitable for automobiles. In both the Sunderbans, and parts of Madagascar, there is some public transport between villages, however once you REALLY enter the mangrove forest, travel is almost exclusively by boat. When deep in the forest, travel happens at a slow pace, on kayaks, rafts and canoes.



Engineering


Among the two communities, the majority of houses are made from mangrove wood. In Madagascar the same mangrove wood is also turned into charcoal, which is the community’s main energy source. To get water, people have to walk to the nearest river or stream, because of the high salinity of the water from the mangrove forest. The people of the Sunderbans however, relied on fishing to make a living, but due to the decreasing fish population, most of the country has turned to shrimp farming.



Environmental Impact

Communities in the Sundarbans and the Madagascar Mangroves heavily rely on the forest for their livelihood. In Madagascar, the trees themselves are the main treasure, providing energy (by turning them into charcoal) and wood. However, in the Madagascar mangrove forests, the trees are heavily exploited for resources. Mangrove exploitation causes soil erosion, subsidence and loss of soil carbon which enters into the atmosphere contributing to pollution and climate change. Many NGOs try to help prevent mangrove deforestation, and help to plant more trees. The people in the Sunderbans have taken advantage of the shrimp population, gathering eggs for shrimp farming, making Bangladesh one of the world’s top shrimp producers. This limits shrimp reproduction and disrupts marine ecosystems. They also need to use salt water which is absorbed by the sand, contaminating the fresh water reserves. But it isn’t just shrimp that gets exploited. During the dry season, one village bustles with 15,000 fishermen who are hogging the fish and drying them for export, further contributing to the destruction of the once thriving fish population.

Production and Exports Since the fish population declined the people in the sunderbans have turned to shrimp farming, producing around 250,000 to 263,000 metric tons of shrimp each year.

 Madagascar exported around $97.2M worth of Crustaceans (shrimp, prawn, lobster, crab etc.) Surprisingly, in 2024, datasets show that Madagascar only exported 20kg worth of charcoal in that entire year. It is absurd how low that number is, despite charcoal being heavily utilized in Madagascar.
Population The current population of the Sundarbans is approximately 7.2 million people. In the Sundarbans children are especially vulnerable to drowning. Studies conclude that around three children, give or take, fall victim to drowning every day. Some of the most inhabited villages in the Sundarbans are Sathkhira (86372), Morelgani (22136), Mongla-Port (60561), Bagerhat (51504).

 In Madagascar, the average life expectancy is 64.3 years. A very unfortunate metric to point out though, is the infant mortality rate. In Madagascar, out of 1000 live births, 42 newborns pass away. For deaths under 5 years old, that number climbs to 62. This is very unfortunate for Madagascar and its citizens, because this developing country does not have as much access to high quality health care and vaccines compared to many other places. The population in Madagascar is clustered in the big city areas, but otherwise the populations are fairly divided. Despite mangroves making a very small percentage of the land in Madagascar, many key cities rely on them. Cities like Mahajanga, Toliara, Morondova and Ambanja make the top 15 in Madagascar population rankings.
Energy In the Sundarbans, the main energy source comes from fuelwood taken from the mangrove forests. This provides heat for cooking and warmth. In Madagascar, the total energy supply comes from various different sources. Nearly all the energy in Madagascar comes from biofuels and waste, with the rest few coming from oils, coal, and the lowest being hydropower. Over 400,000 TJ (terajoules) of electricity have been supplied in Madagascar.
